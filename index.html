The Infrastructure Dividend: Geography, Equity, and the Partisan Myth

An Empirical Analysis of the $1.2 Trillion Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA)

When Congress signed the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act into law in late 2021, it represented the largest federal investment in public works in modern American history. The public narrative promised modernized transit corridors, reinforced bridges, and nationwide high-speed broadband.

Yet two years into execution, a core question remains for taxpayers and policy analysts alike: How is the money actually divided, and do the allocations reflect partisan favoritism under the Biden administration?

By joining official federal agency allocations through March 2023 with 2020 Decennial Census demographics and presidential election results across the 50 states and Washington D.C., this study traces the flow of capital to evaluate allocation parity and political alignment.

1. The Macro Picture: Top-Line Dollars Follow Population Centers

Examining gross allocations produces an immediate pattern: the largest shares go directly to the largest states. California ($18.4B), Texas ($14.2B), New York ($10.1B), Illinois ($8.4B), and Florida ($8.2B) dominate federal outlays.

Because raw funding correlates almost perfectly with population (r = 0.97), aggregate dollar views tell an intuitive story about scale, but mask how resources are distributed across individual residents.

2. The Per Capita Inversion: Small States Win Big

Normalizing funding on a per-capita basis completely flips the geographic narrative. The national average baseline sits at $578 per resident. However, small and sparsely populated states capture outsized shares relative to their tax base.

Alaska ($5,045 per resident), Wyoming ($3,987), Montana ($3,044), and North Dakota ($2,310) lead the nation by wide margins. Conversely, high-growth, densely settled states receive the least support per resident: Florida ($381), North Carolina ($431), Maryland ($437), South Carolina ($449), and California ($465).

3. The Structural Cause: Statutory Formulas Over Demographics

Why does per-capita spending diverge so sharply from population? The driver is statutory formula design. Federal highway and infrastructure grants incorporate baseline state minimums alongside weights for total land area, lane miles, and bridge conditions.

Plotting state population on a logarithmic scale against per-resident funding demonstrates that geography, not partisan politics, governs allocation volume (r = -0.61). Sparsely populated jurisdictions consistently cluster at the high end regardless of whether they voted for Biden (Vermont, D.C., Rhode Island) or Trump (Wyoming, Alaska, Montana, the Dakotas).

4. The Partisan Test: Red States Capture Greater Per-Capita Funding

If the executive branch steered funds to reward Democratic constituencies or protect battleground positions, Biden-won states and close swing states should exhibit elevated per-capita spending.

The data refutes this premise. Trump-won states received $627 per resident across the aggregate population cohort, compared to $542 for Biden-won states—an 16% advantage for Republican-voting states. Highly contested battleground states (decided by under 5 percentage points) received the lowest cohort allocation at $476 per resident.

Evaluating the continuous margin of victory confirms the absence of partisan bias. Rank correlation between Biden’s margin and per-capita infrastructure dollars is mildly negative (rs = -0.27). Rather than steering capital toward political allies, the distribution reflects the intersection of statutory funding minimums with America's rural political geography.

Analytical Findings & Methodological Scope

  • Population Equity: IIJA funds are not distributed equitably on a per-person basis. Per-capita funding spans a 13-fold range, from $381 in Florida to $5,045 in Alaska.
  • Partisan Alignment: Empirical evidence contradicts claims of executive steering or partisan patronage. Trump-won states captured an average of $627 per person versus $542 in Biden-won states. Formula structures built into statute grant equal baseline allocations regardless of population mass, naturally elevating rural states.
  • Data Coverage & Boundaries: Formula funds reflect statutory allocations as of March 2023. This dataset does not capture discretionary, competitive agency grants where executive administration discretion could play an operational role. U.S. territories and tribal nations are excluded from political comparisons due to electoral college status.
